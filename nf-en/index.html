Last year for your birthday I wrote about the Ukrainian rapper Alyona Alyona, and now it's fast hip-hop again (what else would you expect from me), but in a completely different mood: as life-affirming as Alyona is, NF is that bleak.

Nathan Feuerstein is a white American rapper whose albums top the Billboard chart. He's very lyric-driven: in an era when you can repeat "gucci gang" ten times, he writes long, thoughtful lyrics that show off a rich vocabulary. And he obsesses over rhymes and sound so much that his obsessive-compulsive disorder clearly plays a part. Performing these lyrics, he sometimes switches to machine-gun speed. As for content, you'll find both dark soul-searching and self-praise. In the second case, he brags about skill rather than wealth, as many rappers do: luxury doesn't interest him, and having become a star, he still wears simple clothes.

Replace the artist's name in the previous paragraph with "Eminem", and the whole paragraph still holds true. No wonder NF hasn't been able to escape comparisons with him for his entire career. And Eminem himself seems to have taken a shot at Nathan with the line "an effin' Recovery clone of me" in "The Ringer" (he didn't name anyone directly, but the line hardly starts with "an ef" by accident). But calling him "an Eminem clone" drives me up the wall. Yes, the huge influence is plain to see, and NF talks about it openly in every interview. But reducing his music to that influence alone, in my view, means missing the most important thing in it and overlooking important differences.

The most obvious difference: NF never swears. And yet many of his tracks come down on the listener like an avalanche, and in 2020 making bangers like that without swearing is like boxing with your hands tied (and winning). "I can't believe there's someone that can rap so hard without cussing", reads a comment under his video for "The Search", and that comment has 35 thousand likes. Meanwhile, I can't think offhand of a single Eminem song that streaming services don't mark as "explicit"; for him, it's a key ingredient (to quote the man himself, "from the moment I heard rap was cussing, I was in").

The second important difference (which also explains the lack of swearing): NF is a Christian, and that matters for understanding his music too. I don't think it's right to call it Christian hip-hop, since religion isn't the focus here, but it comes up often enough for Billboard to include NF in its Christian charts (where, by sales, he turns out to be a major figure). Whereas Eminem, even with all the swearing removed from his lyrics, is impossible to imagine as a Christian star.

You could also point out that the beats are noticeably different, and that Nathan sings his own hooks. But I think none of that is the main thing; something else matters more. Yes, Eminem has his soul-searching too, describing his inner demons, with titles like "Darkness". But, as the saying goes, "you merely adopted the dark; I was born in it, moulded by it". For NF, all-consuming hopelessness isn't just one of his themes; it's where he spends most of his time and what most of his lyrics are about. His songs are fifty shades of gloom, and his videos are fifty shades of washed-out grey. Pitchfork wrote that one of his albums "often reads like a suicide note".

But where Pitchfork, for some reason, concludes "the album is weak", I feel the opposite. There are cases when an artist is obviously not very compatible with the world around them, and it's clearly very hard on them, but at the same time that very thing gives rise to striking work. They become a raw nerve that takes in all the world's sorrow and relays it to us. The result is uncomfortable but powerful music. For me, NF is one of those cases.

What sets him apart from other such cases is that he has not only an "everything is bad" side, but also a battle-rap side. Being a Christian, he doesn't diss everyone directly the way Eminem does, but asserting himself through rap skills is something close to his heart. And even when a lyric has no outright self-praise, you can see how much technical delivery and well-crafted lyrics matter to him. He makes songs about how terrible everything is with a jeweler's care, and clearly sees beauty in that care. It seems to be one of the few things in life that bring him joy, and music literally saves him from death.

And maybe not only him. NF's music resonates with people who feel bad for all kinds of reasons, and in the comments you often read something like "finally someone put my feelings into words, thank you, it makes me feel a little better". This resonance is most visible with the song "Hate Myself", because people who type "I hate myself" into Google stumble upon it. Someone also uploaded an hour-long version of the song for those tired of hitting "replay" on the regular video, and the comments there say "probly safe to assume everyone that came here wants to die".

In essence, Nathan uses music as therapy. In fact, one of his albums is called "Therapy Session", so he draws this parallel himself. Of course, you'd want to advise someone with such inner struggles to turn not only to his fans but also to real therapists who can offer more qualified help. But he understands this himself and did so long ago.

The main drawback of NF's music is clear: many will call it monotonous. Eminem, again, can be goofy, aggressive, sad, motivating and much else. Nathan exists within a much narrower range of moods, and across four albums there's no escaping a sense of repetition. That's why, even though I like the artist, I still haven't listened to all four albums in full.

Still, there's variety here, and some songs stand out noticeably to me. I've already shared two of them above: the first tries to knock you off your feet with sheer force, keeps shifting gears and has no chorus; the second, on the contrary, has a soft, melodic chorus. I think both are really great in their own way, and after the pandemic I want to sing both at karaoke.

And I'll finish with a third one, different from both: it's a rare case when an NF song can cautiously be called optimistic. Well, at least not pessimistic. So it's probably the best fit for a birthday!
