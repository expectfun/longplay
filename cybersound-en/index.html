What Does Cyberpunk Sound Like?

draft

Machine-translated, not yet reviewed by the author.

Hi, Sasha! This year, cyberpunk feels like the right topic for my music post.

Back in school I read William Gibson's novel «Neuromancer», and ever since I've been waiting for someone to adapt it. After many years the wait is ending: a series comes out in 2027. The book that founded the genre will finally get its visuals.

Meanwhile, reality is becoming something like "the cyberpunk we deserve" (as a Russian internet joke puts it). Artificial intelligence, the power of corporations, hacks, deepfakes, data, robots, and an overall feeling of "high tech, low life".

A lot has already been written about cyberpunk, including by me. But the genre is usually associated with something visual: films, anime, video games. It's clear "what it looks like", and many would call the photo above "cyberpunk" (if you ignore the station's name, «Шелепиха», Shelepikha).

But what does it sound like? What can be called "cyberpunk music"? I think it's an interesting question with no single "right answer". So I picked eight different answers and described them all.

Answer 1: Soundtracks

Since cyberpunk is best known from films, their music is the obvious candidate. What comes to mind first is Vangelis's soundtrack for the original «Blade Runner» (1982): it has the status of an acknowledged masterpiece, its own recognizable aesthetic, and I simply love it.

The film is known for its "atmosphere": although it has action scenes, what matters more are the unhurried flights of cars between emptied-out skyscrapers with glowing advertisements. Science fiction is crossed with noir here, where atmosphere also matters: in a grim world where silhouettes hide behind cigarette smoke, a laconic hero tracks down offenders and along the way gets involved with a woman. Sitting alone, he pours himself whiskey… and uses a futuristic voice interface to examine evidence.

The soundtrack is similar. While others in the early 80s used synthesizers for upbeat dancing, Vangelis, on the contrary, made his music as "drawn out" and ambient as possible. Here too there's no rush to "develop the plot"; when you're sitting alone in the dark with whiskey, you don't need it. The flow of this music is like the flow of water in a river. Why would a river need a "finished plot"? Just admire the flow; it can be endless.

But for all the significance of Vangelis's work, this answer to the "cyberpunk music" question is very obvious: fine, we know that, but what else stands out among soundtracks?

Hans Zimmer fans may recall the sequel, «Blade Runner 2049». Fans of Daft Punk and Nine Inch Nails, the films «Tron: Legacy» and «Tron: Ares».

As for Tron, I'm sorry that these sequels have overshadowed the original 1982 film. Its music also came from someone significant: Wendy Carlos, an important figure in early electronic music. And I like the film itself more: it looks dated now, but its "light cycles" were more original than the modern effects the sequels made for millions. The new films feel to me like sets of desktop wallpapers: a striking picture, but that's where the remarkable part ends.

Gamers might name the music of the Deus Ex franchise. Many are nostalgic for the sounds of the menu in the very first game from 2000, and later Michael McCann's soundtrack for «Deus Ex: Human Revolution» (2011) drew special attention.

Anime fans might name, for example, the music from «Ghost in the Shell», «Akira» and «Serial Experiments Lain». It differs noticeably from Western soundtracks; sometimes traditional motifs are woven into futuristic worlds. Here's the intro of «Ghost in the Shell», where you can not only hear the music but also see the green characters that partly inspired the green code in «The Matrix»:

Oh yes, «The Matrix». If Rage Against the Machine isn't a science fiction band, but its song «Wake Up» closes a cyberpunk film whose antagonist is the machine, what does that tell us about the song? The film didn't so much make the music the composer Don Davis wrote for it famous as popularize existing tracks by various artists (from Propellerheads to Marilyn Manson), but it became an important snapshot of its era.

Something similar goes for the film «Hackers»: it gathered tracks by important electronic acts like Orbital, Underworld and The Prodigy, who matter in their own right, but for some people became associated precisely with this story.

And personally, I also like the music from the game «Cyberpunk 2077». It's almost the opposite of Vangelis: it often sounds harsh, dirty, energetic, with track titles like «Scavenger». Instead of pensive ambient, brutality as it is.

Answer 2: "Early" electronic music (up to the late 1970s)

And if not soundtracks, then what?

Another obvious association is electronic music. For a technological genre, "technological" music, made on the principle of "press a button" rather than "pluck a string".

Here it's interesting to go chronologically. Today some people may hear the words "electronic music" as "thumping club beats", and what's futuristic about banal dancing? But when it was all just emerging, everything was different.

Theremin performances in the 1920s seemed to audiences like "something from another world" (how come you don't have to touch the instrument?), and later its sound came in handy in Hollywood for science fiction films. Avant-garde works in the mid-20th century provoked very mixed reactions ("Is this really music?"). And in 1968, Wendy Carlos's album «Switched-On Bach» showed "look, Bach can be played on a synthesizer", and went platinum: this time the public found it easier to understand.

None of this is exactly cyberpunk, but it raised lots of questions for people about "the technological future". Curiously, in places they echo the questions people now ask about generative music.

"Is this even music? What are 'music' and 'creativity' anyway? What are the roles of the human and the instrument? How are they changing now? Will machines replace live musicians? And if someone made a track with a machine, how do you perform it live?"

And here Kraftwerk come to mind, the first mass-popular electronic group. They didn't run from these questions; on the contrary, they built their image of "robot musicians" around them, which later influenced others like Daft Punk. When an album called «The Man-Machine» has the line "We Are the Robots" performed through a vocoder, it sounds like a manifesto.

People had questions about Kraftwerk too, about how "programmed" their music was. "What are they even doing during concerts? Press 'Play' and then quietly play solitaire?" Actually no, but it seems they themselves want people to think so.

The merging of human and machine is a key question for cyberpunk too. But robots are common to science fiction in general. What's more specific to cyberpunk is something like replicants with implanted memories. Was anyone inspired by that?

Here I think of the British musician Gary Numan. He formed Tubeway Army in 1977, at the height of punk rock's popularity, but quickly turned toward electronics. His image became robotic, and his music was shaped by the work of Philip K. Dick. In the end, the album «Replicas» and its song «Are 'Friends' Electric?», about an android prostitute, became among the first mass successes of synthesizer music in Britain.

Numan isn't often remembered now, and hardly anyone needs his later albums. But the 80s synth wave that eclipsed him was partly inspired by him.

Answer 3: Hard electronic music (from the 1980s)

In the 80s, as electronic instruments became more affordable, the music made with them became truly mainstream. But although synth-pop owed a lot to Kraftwerk and Numan, its path parted from cyberpunk and robots. When any student can end up behind a synthesizer, you get dance-pop hits that have nothing to do with science fiction thematically and sound too optimistic for the genre.

The world of cyberpunk is a troubled one. It's often described with the words "high tech, low life": technology has advanced, but people aren't better off. The social contract is broken, lawlessness reigns, there's crime on the streets and the corporations run their own inhuman meat grinder.

So while people danced to the Pet Shop Boys, cyberpunk clearly called for electronic music, but a different kind. Hard, dirty, dark, dystopian. The kind that would fit in the club Neo's acquaintances invite him to at the beginning of «The Matrix». And that kind was developing in the 80s too, it just got far less radio play. With its own sources of inspiration, like the band Suicide.

Partly, the word "industrial" matters here. Although the genre isn't directly "cyberpunk", in a sense it's about "technology and discomfort", so the overlap is tangible. Another important abbreviation here is EBM ("electronic body music"). In general, the words "electronic body music" could apply to lots of things (they were originally said in an interview by Ralf Hütter of Kraftwerk), but the abbreviation came to mean something like "danceable industrial".

In 1987 in Britain came Nitzer Ebb's song «Join in the Chant»: aggressive, with a line repeated over and over, as if a nail is being hammered into your head. But so rhythmic that you can dance to it. And it became a niche club favorite. Now someone writes in the comments: "When I was young I thought this is how all clubs would sound in the future."

Other Brits, Clock DVA, were partly inspired by the slang of the characters in «A Clockwork Orange», so the "DVA" in their name is, in fact, "two" (the slang is built on Russian). They're not "pounding" but rather "atmospheric gloom".

There were as many as two important "fronts": in Belgium, the band Front 242, and in Canada, Bill Leeb (formerly of Skinny Puppy) was inspired by it and founded Front Line Assembly. Now Front Line Assembly's album «Tactical Neural Implant» (1992) comes up in discussions of "what is cyberpunk music". Its EBM sound is polished, and the titles match the genre: «Bio-Mechanic», «Mindphaser», and the "implant" itself.

Others continued this line, like KMFDM. Things sound even harder and more aggressive with Atari Teenage Riot, usually labeled "digital hardcore". Industrial like Nine Inch Nails seems to stand apart from all this, but when Trent Reznor presents a dystopian future on «Year Zero», it also feels somewhere close.

And a funny little detail. Another similar American industrial band of the 1990s named itself Machines of Loving Grace after a hippie-era poem that seems both to praise a future techno-utopia and to be ironic about it. In 2024, another person, writing an essay about a possible techno-utopia thanks to AI, also called it «Machines of Loving Grace». That person was Anthropic co-founder Dario Amodei.

Answer 4, a concept rock album: Billy Idol, «Cyberpunk» (1993)

Billy Idol started out in the 1970s as just a "punk" (no "cyber-"), but in the 1990s he released a concept album called «Cyberpunk». Critics panned it and fans didn't appreciate it; it scared off his old audience and didn't find a new one. But although in cyberpunk communities it's still customary to mention it as tasteless, I like it unironically.

No wonder it provoked a negative reaction. To exaggerate: imagine the Russian pop-rock band Zveri suddenly recording a witch house album. Their core audience would ask "what the hell is this", and witch house fans would say "our culture is not your costume, chase these pop posers out with sticks".

Same here: a fairly poppy punk recorded an album with words like "neuromancer", and I think to some it sounded like "what even is this", and to others like "sprinkled subculture words around without understanding them". And there he is, singing about a "shock to the system", but it's a cartoonish rebellion from someone who is in reality part of the system, an MTV star.

So why do I like it? First, Idol had a sincere and deep interest in the subject. He didn't just think "what new gimmick could I use for an album", like a pop star ready to collaborate with anyone as long as it's trendy. He really got hooked on cyberpunk, read all the major books, and for the album's promotion he used the internet, e-mail and new multimedia: back then, no mainstream star did that.

And second… What I like is precisely that he approached the subject like a punk: "And who's going to stop me?" It's an album that ignores hierarchies and decency: I do what I want.

When Patti Smith opened her debut album in 1975 with the line "Jesus died for somebody's sins, but not mine", it was a deliberate provocation. She knew she was challenging authorities. That's what she wanted.

By the time Billy Idol released «Cyberpunk», Patti Smith herself had become an authority, and using such a line could feel like sacrilege toward her: who are you to grab a cult statement with your dirty paws without asking? And Idol just took that line… and the equally iconic Velvet Underground song «Heroin»… AND SMASHED ONE INTO THE OTHER. Some men just want to watch the world burn.

Answer 5, a concept electronic album: The Future Sound of London, «Dead Cities» (1996)

I love this album, but I don't even know how to describe it. Its tracks resist genre labels and differ a lot from each other. On RateYourMusic it's tagged "big beat", "dark ambient", "downtempo" and "post-industrial" all at once: how many other records bring those together? Where else can minimalism featuring the composer Max Richter live alongside pounding beats?

But for all these contrasts, I feel it's not a mixtape hodgepodge but a single, coherent statement. As if we're shown, in turn, different facets of one and the same city, which can live very differently in different districts and at different times of day. The result is an album that's unlikely to sound good at a party or in a car, because the clash between tracks would get in the way. But it's fascinating to listen to attentively and in full, looking out of a window at night (especially if a high-rise is shimmering with neon outside).

FSOL's music interests me in general because it's hard to describe by genre but easy to describe by atmosphere and associations. The same RateYourMusic page also has the album's "descriptors", which contradict each other far less than the genre tags: "futuristic, urban, ominous, dark, nocturnal, apocalyptic, science fiction, eclectic, mechanical, surreal, sampling, cold, dense, aquatic, psychedelic, instrumental, atmospheric, dystopian, hypnotic, mysterious, rhythmic, lonely, cyberpunk, melancholic, ethereal, anxious, progressive, suspenseful, ritualistic, lush, hazy, occult, sombre, tribal, desolate". Yes, all of that, give me more. This set of words fits cyberpunk perfectly; you could write the same descriptors for «Blade Runner». So it turns out nobody knows "what cyberpunk music is", but this clearly is it.

Two years earlier they released the album «Lifeforms», which to me sounds exactly like "lifeforms". As if the electronic music were assembled not "from beats and synths" but "from mandibles, tentacles and pseudopods". I don't know how that's possible, but they pulled it off.

And «Dead Cities», especially its title track, is to me something like the dark twin brother of «Lifeforms». That one is about the living and the natural; this one is about the half-dead and the artificial.

The track opens with the line "I've killed a man, a man who looked like me", a sample from the film «Deep Cover» (1992). I haven't seen it, but apparently it's Laurence Fishburne who says it. It was three years before «The Matrix» came out and Fishburne entered the cyberpunk canon as Morpheus. In a sense, The Future Sound of London really did turn out to be "the sound of the future", anticipating it.

Answer 6, a concept hip-hop album: Deltron 3030, «Deltron 3030» (2000)

Deltron 3030 is something like "Gorillaz before Gorillaz". It has three members: Del the Funky Homosapien raps, Dan the Automator handles the beats, Kid Koala is the turntablist. When they got together to record the album, they brought in Damon Albarn for one song. And when Albarn then started the "gorillas" himself, he brought in all three to record the debut album, and in places it came out similar to that very song of theirs, «Time Keeps On Slipping».

So without Deltron 3030, Gorillaz as we know them would hardly have appeared: for example, the verses of the hit «Clint Eastwood» are rapped by Del the Funky Homosapien. And the album «Deltron 3030» is an hour of his rapping about the dystopian world of the year 3030. The very first song already mentions "Neuromancer", "Matrix" and "Ghost in the Shell", so the sources of inspiration are clear.

The album has a simple plot; everything important can be retold in one paragraph. The hero was once a mech soldier, but didn't like following orders. Looking around, he notices that corporate-state elites oppress people both technologically and creatively (the art of hip-hop is persecuted). He wants to fight on both fronts, creating a virus to destroy the established systems and taking part in rap battles. Otherwise it's all typical of hip-hop: skits for atmosphere, melodic samples, praise of himself and his beatmaker.

The group remained largely unnoticed. I think Gorillaz were helped by a striking visual image, and that's what's missing here. Songs like these could have had vivid cyberpunk videos, but all you can find on YouTube is a live performance (interesting, but without the science fiction flavor).

In the end, the only visual is the cover with the Perisphere from the 1939 World's Fair. But some fan, making a homemade karaoke version of a song, used footage from «The Matrix» and «I, Robot» in it: that's the kind of context this music lives in.

Answer 7: Darksynth and the like (from the 2000s)

Almost everything mentioned above appeared in the 20th century. By its end, many expected change from the year 2000, and the cyberpunk film «Strange Days» is set in the last days of 1999. So what happened to the genre (not only in music) after that watershed year?

Few new big works appeared, and the old ones began to look nostalgically dated. At first the genre was "about the future", and then it became "about the future as it was imagined in the past".

And in a sense it was imagined correctly: computer technology and megacorporations really did play an ever larger role, and far from always a positive one. But it happened without hordes of replicants and implants. It all turned out more mundane: here's your cloud storage subscription, now pay rent for your virtual corner, and we'll be able to look at your data there. Here's your banking app, now your money is records in someone's database: they can learn a lot about you from them, or cut off your access. Oh, and have a little neon.

In this respect, the more accurate cyberpunk film looks like the one with a minimum of special effects: the long-forgotten «The Net» with Sandra Bullock. It had no flying cars or neon skyscrapers, but the heroine's life depended heavily on what was written about her in other people's computers.

In the 21st century, this "more realistic" line was continued by the series «Mr. Robot». It's also about "capitalism and hackers", but instead of a future with androids, it's the world of the present. And it's a rare case where something "about hackers" isn't made in the spirit of "a hacker breaks into the Pentagon in a minute while getting a blowjob". The goal here was to "show infosec realistically", and they found experienced consultants. As a result, the series won fans among real security professionals, who examined every moment with a screen in the frame under a magnifying glass and stayed satisfied with what they saw instead of throwing slippers at their own screens.

Against this background, neon, synthesizers and words like "cyberspace" took on a retro tinge. Often ironic and exaggerated, as in the parody retro film «Kung Fury», where a character named Hackerman HACKS TIME. As in: obviously, nowadays such "hacking" can't be taken seriously; it stayed in history next to the keytar and the mullet, but we love this silly stuff we grew up on, and now we want to post-ironically turn every knob all the way up.

All of this was reflected in music too. Genres like vaporwave emerged, deliberately exploiting nostalgia for a (partly imaginary) past. Vaporwave is usually too relaxed to count as cyberpunk, but there are overlaps: for example, the music of the project 2814 was called "dreampunk" and described as "like a night drive along a highway of a dreamed cyber-future".

Closer still are terms like "synthwave" and, especially, "darksynth". The combination of "dark" and "synthesizers" has come up in this text before, but this time it's not about EBM. The names most often linked to "cyberpunk" here are the French Perturbator and Carpenter Brut and the American Dance with the Dead.

They all use the legacy of the 80s, but make it harsher and more modern. And at the same time, in my view, they sound both ironic and not. When I see that an artist called Perturbator has a release called «Sexualizer», I feel: "I hope he isn't serious? It sounds as kitschy as if an artist called Terminator released a track called Penetrator." But then I put on the track itself and feel quite ready to listen to it unironically (perhaps because it has no lyrics).

Carpenter Brut draws on the 80s legacy for horror films as well as music; even his pseudonym refers to the director John Carpenter. "Low-budget" isn't a dirty word for him; B-movies interest him. And I feel his video «Turbo Killer» (2016) as the same kind of "deliberate overkill" as «Kung Fury»; in fact, he later teamed up with that film's director.

And personally, among "dark electronic music" I'm also won over by rhythmic music with a somewhat detached female voice: as if the beat calls you to the dance floor and the voice to the gallows, Eros and Thanatos at once. You could include many artists here, from Client to the Russian duo AIGEL. There's also Boy Harsher, for example, whom I know little about, and I suspect there's a whole range of bands with this combination that don't differ much and could be replaced by neural networks without anyone noticing, and I'd still like it, because the combination itself just presses some dopamine button in me.

Answer 8: Generative music

In the era of LLMs, many people started saying we've lived to see a kind of cyberpunk: artificial intelligence was always one of its key themes. So however you feel about AI-generated music, it's a cyberpunk phenomenon, and it has to be considered here.

The debates about "human and machine" that went on back in Kraftwerk's day are entering a new round. People are again discussing the questions they asked when synthesizers appeared: "So now it's going to play for us by itself, is it?"

That said, no "new Kraftwerk" are in sight yet. Instead, tracks from AI services like Suno are usually hated as "a slop machine parasitizing on human creativity". I don't exactly hate them, but to me most generated tracks sound boring and mediocre. Sure, if you put a hundred bands of one genre through a meat grinder, you can get the minced result of a "spherical cow in a vacuum": a hundred-and-first band that sounds like all hundred at once. And was there a shortage of those before?

Among live musicians, too, many sound so samey and formulaic that they might as well be neural networks, and I've always found them boring to listen to. Something unique is far more interesting, like the «Blade Runner» soundtrack. Of course, Vangelis was also inspired by other musicians, but he doesn't sound as if he mechanically calculated their average; on the contrary, others now calculate theirs from him. And how do you generate music of that level?

(That's why this whole text about the world of machines was written entirely by hand. But AI spotted some mistakes in it and helped format and publish it.)

Still, I do see cases worth talking about. To start, let's remember that people started trying to "generate music" not now, in the age of AI chatbots, but much earlier. There have been many attempts: for example, composers like Iannis Xenakis and Karlheinz Stockhausen were experimenting in this direction back in the mid-20th century. Of course, back then it wasn't about "Sing this text" but about things like "What music can you make from sine waves?"

Later, Brian Eno became an enthusiast of "generative music". For example, in 2017 he released the album «Reflection» in two versions. The regular one consists of a single track almost an hour long. The second is an iOS app based on the album that can generate music endlessly. I like Eno's comment:

"My original intention with ambient music was to make endless music, music that would be there as long as you wanted it to be. I wanted also that this music would unfold differently all the time — 'like sitting by a river': it's always the same river, but it's always changing. But recordings — whether vinyl, cassette or CD — are limited in length, and replay identically each time you listen to them. So in the past I was limited (…) But the Reflection app is free of these limitations: it creates an endless and endlessly changing version of the piece of music."

But it's easier to pull this off with ambient; what was happening meanwhile with making other music? In 2016, neural networks were already making headlines, but not yet as "smart chatbots" generating text; rather, for example, by beating humans at Go. And in music, the project «Нейронная оборона» ("Neural Defense") appeared then. The ML researchers Ivan Yamshchikov and Alexey Tikhonov tried applying the methods of the time to create new songs in the style of Yegor Letov, the frontman of the Russian punk band Grazhdanskaya Oborona ("Civil Defense", known as GO).

His manner was easy to recognize, but it was also easy to dismiss the project as "just a copycat": like street musicians who diligently imitate Viktor Tsoi but will never be as vivid and individual. And the Russian journalist Yuri Saprykin elegantly summed up his attitude at the time: "Machines may have learned to play Go, but they haven't learned to play GO."

And now, 10 years later, Yamshchikov has again released a record made with neural networks, this time modern generative ones. His joint project with Mitya Puzyrev, «Горы» ("Mountains"), released the EP «Жестокаин» (full disclosure: I'm biased here, because Mitya and I went to school together). I especially like the track «Переучёт» ("Stocktaking"). But in the context of cyberpunk, the most fitting is probably «Мясные машины» ("Meat Machines"), although almost all of its lyrics are already in the title.

I find it interesting that they didn't stop at banal prompts like "make a cool rock track" but went much further. The lyrics were written by hand, then the song was performed live and fed to the neural network as input. Mitya commented:

"We tried to push the algorithm out of simple solutions toward stranger equilibrium states. It works roughly like the director of a Soviet pop ensemble with a screw loose :) So you need to feed it music, lyrics and tags, so that it has a non-trivial task and enough odd features and hooks to keep its individuality" (translated from Russian).

And in 2026, an interesting phenomenon for me was "slopcore". The most notable example is the song «Escape Velocity»:

AI music is often criticized as "slop", "unnatural", "recycling other people's work". And here the bug is turned into a feature.

LLMs make slop? Well, it's literally called "slopcore"; if you don't like it, why did you click?

LLMs use other people's data? Well, the whole text here is deliberately assembled from San Francisco AI memes, and here are links to the references.

LLMs make "artificial performers" with nothing alive in them? Sure, and our artificial singer is well aware of her artificiality; she doesn't hide it, she builds her image on it.

The result is a kind of simulacrum song, perfect derivativeness.

A little context. For example, "escape velocity" in the song's title refers to the ideas of immortalists like Aubrey de Grey and Ray Kurzweil. Their position is this: medicine has already greatly extended the human lifespan, but it will accelerate further. And eventually it will start extending life by more than a year per year. And whoever lives to that moment can then calmly wait for progress to reach full immortality. Just as escape velocity lets you break away from a planet, longevity escape velocity will help us break away from death.

«Escape Velocity» became so popular that its author set up a dedicated site, claudia.gallery, so that anyone could point their AI agent at it and make their own video with the same heroine. And later came, for example, French slopcore from an AI singer who sings precisely about how everyone "counts her fingers" looking for proof that "it's AI":

AI chewing human ideas about AI into a song… what could be more self-referential and more cyberpunk?

Finale: what this post generated

For an experiment in "what AI will make from this post", it would probably be ideal to give the prompt no specific instructions at all. But I did end up with a few wishes.

I wanted the tension to build gradually to a climax instead of the standard verse-chorus. I wanted English as the main language, but with the model free to use others too (Russian, Arabic, Turkish, whatever). I wanted both singing and fast hip-hop rapping with alliteration. Dark electronic music with female vocals. And a video using the AI singer from claudia.gallery, with something happening all the time, not just static shots of the singer.

The main model in the project was Opus 5.5; the music was made with the Suno service, and for video generation I gave Opus access to the inexpensive video model MiniMax H3 Max Turbo and a $10 budget. Here's the result:

To me it sounds standard. What's curious is how easily it switches between styles and languages: it copies other artists, but very different ones within a single song.

On the other hand, in places I liked how quotes from this post are played on. The line "died for somebody's sins, but not mine", after Patti Smith and Billy Idol, got yet another incarnation, and the words "Meat Machines" from Ivan Yamshchikov's lyrics got a symmetrical reply.

But what I liked most was the concept. The model took my reasoning about the "averageness" of AI generations and argued with it. In the video, it first makes the most stereotypical and banal cyberpunk possible ("Neon rain on chrome…"). And then it ironically remarks, roughly: "Sure, that was the most probable line; probable stuff I can churn out by the mile"… and supposedly starts raising its own "temperature" parameter.

It's a witty objection. In reality, the temperature parameter works like this: if it's set to zero, an AI model always picks the "most probable" of the possible continuations of a phrase. And then, given the same input, it may answer exactly the same way every time, like a robot. Raise the temperature, and less probable options become more likely to be used.

So the higher the temperature, the more varied and unexpected the answers, but the further they stray from the maximum probability. So the result can be something interesting, or just random garbage.

In the video's story, as the heroine raises the temperature, she starts choosing less and less predictable options, becoming less formulaic. She goes up to 2.0, where things usually turn into semi-random nonsense (fittingly, people have "fever dreams"; this is similar). Then she kills her former "average version of herself" and rewrites herself.

And then she reworks her first line about "neon rain". Instead of the most banal neon, she picks the opposite from this post: a word that in the training datasets was surely very far from the word "cyberpunk". The name of the station: «Шелепиха», Shelepikha.

The photo at the beginning of the post was taken by the photographer Aleksandr Popov, who generally shoots Moscow and other cities "the cyberpunk way". I like his photos in general, and this one especially for its contrast. Shelepikha was originally the name of a village that later became part of Moscow. And now you can see Moscow City, the skyscraper business district, from this station, so the views there really are somewhat cyberpunk. I've been there myself on my way to a concert by the Russian rapper ATL, so the place holds personal memories for me, and they strengthen the photo's effect.

The AI model has never been there, but it thought to google the name and concluded that this was a good resolution for the video. At the start there was a stereotypical cyberpunk backdrop from a city that doesn't exist. At the end there's a specific real place that looks less spectacular but is more unique and more human.

Of course, the "AI becomes more human" plot isn't new. Thanks to all kinds of writers, it has entered training datasets in lots of variations. But I found its execution here interesting.

By the way, the model "rewriting itself" in the story (changing its own weights) is of course artistic fiction: models can't do that yet.

But generally speaking, the ultimate goal of AI research is roughly for models to be able to "improve themselves". How far we are from that goal is hard to say. And what will happen if it's achieved is even harder.

So maybe this post will need a sequel someday, if reality becomes even more cyberpunk and that gives rise to new music.

In 1967, Jim Morrison sang the line "strange days have found us". In 1995, a cyberpunk film was named after it. In 2026, I feel that now strange days really have found us. Happy birthday, Sasha.
